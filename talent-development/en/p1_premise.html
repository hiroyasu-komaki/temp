PART I　PREMISE

Why Strategy B

Strategy B is not an alternative to Strategy A. It is the precondition that keeps Strategy A working.
This part sets out the reasoning.

01The capability gap — three lines

Almost every discussion about capability reduces to three lines.

Required capability (target line)
The standard the organisation must reach and then sustain in order to keep delivering results. It is set by where the business is going, and cannot be moved by effort on the capability side.
Current level (grey line)
Without deliberate learning, the relative value of a skill declines as the environment changes. Doing nothing does not hold the line flat — it bends it downwards.
Skill gap
The area between the two lines. It is what must be closed, and equally what must be measured.
First implication of the figure
If nothing is done, the gap widens by itself.
Because the current line slopes downwards, the gap keeps growing even when the target line is flat. "Maintaining the status quo" is therefore not an available option. What feels like holding steady is relative decline.
02There are two strategies for closing the gap

The two growth curves in the figure correspond to two modes of acquiring capability. Asking which is better is not a valid question. They differ in how fast they start and where they stop.

03Strategy A — buying speed and time

Strategy A must not be treated as a closed chapter. It is the route we currently depend on, and a means we will continue to use within limits. Without understanding how it works, everything that follows collapses into the crude reading that "external dependence is bad".

Strategy A is a transformation-and-acquisition approach for closing a severe skill gap in a short period.

3.1　The growth trajectory and the mechanism of the plateau

The defining feature of Strategy A is the explosive take-off visible as soon as the measure begins. Because fully formed capability is brought in from outside, the organisation approaches the target level rapidly and sees visible results immediately.

Beyond a certain point, however, the curve flattens, and an early plateau is its inherent fate.

The plateau is not caused by the quality of the external source. Knowledge and systems brought in from outside collide with existing organisational processes and culture, or internal proficiency in using them fails to keep pace. Both are phenomena that occur on the receiving side.

3.2　Five measures for transplanting capability from outside

This strategy works by buying or borrowing capability the organisation does not have.

What we have used over the long term is the third (→ Ch. 4). And the reason this framework places training in a supporting role is that the fifth belongs to Strategy A — it leaves no traces, and therefore does not accumulate (→ Part VI, Ch. 33).

3.3　The absorptive-capacity ceiling — the receiver decides how high it goes

What determines the success and the ceiling of Strategy A is not so much the quality of the external source as the receiving side's absorptive capacity.

Absorptive capacity
The organisational ability to identify external knowledge, assimilate and transform it into one's own context, and finally apply it in a form that produces returns.
However advanced the capability brought in from outside, if the organisation's receiving apparatus — communication, evaluation practices, mechanisms for internalisation — is inadequate, the acquired capability stays borrowed, becomes obsolete, and is swallowed by the grey line: the relative decline of the current level.
Execute Strategy A without a receiving apparatus and the grey line keeps falling. The phenomenon of investing while the gap widens occurs by exactly this route.

3.4　Strategic significance and risk for a global HQ

For an organisation overseeing three sites, Strategy A is critical for responding to discontinuous change. Shifts such as AI and cyber threats do not wait for the pace of internal accumulation.

Content
Significance — relieving urgencyLeaving a severe skill gap unaddressed erodes competitive position quickly. Strategy A first pushes the organisation past the immediate target level and buys the time needed to survive
Risk — variance between regionsAbsorptive capacity and receptiveness differ by site. Even a uniform Pareto-type measure led by HQ will produce different take-off speeds and different degrees of eventual embedding at each site

The second row is precisely what this figure calls the "regional adaptation gap". The principle that we do not apply a single uniform standard across the three sites is our response to that structure (→ Part II, Ch. 8).

3.5　Conclusion — Strategy A buys time

Where Strategy A stands
It is an extremely powerful means of buying speed and time. On its own, however, it rarely becomes a durable advantage.
Whether the organisation can use the time bought by Strategy A to build, in parallel, the foundation of Strategy B — converting externally obtained knowledge into a strength specific to the organisation — is the real heart of the matter.

That sentence fixes the position of this entire document. The next chapter asks what we have done with the time we bought.

04We have already executed Strategy A

This is the starting point of the document, and the point at which the discussion turns from the general to our own situation.

The Japan site has supported the business for several decades without ever having enough headcount to cover every domain, and has therefore been dependent on vendors over the long term. In terms of §3.2, this is the third measure — external vendors and consultants — run not as a series of projects but as a permanent operating model.

And it has worked. Without the headcount to cover every domain, the organisation has kept the entire business value chain running without interruption. Given that this held for decades, the choice should be judged as having been correct.

The question is not whether Strategy A failed

What Strategy A buys is time. The question about our own situation therefore reduces to one point.

What did we do with the time that Strategy A has been buying us for decades?

Had the foundation of Strategy B — a mechanism for converting externally obtained knowledge into an organisation-specific strength — been built in parallel, capability would now be accumulated. Yet we do not even possess the means to confirm whether it has been (→ Part II, Ch. 10).

Was it never built, or was it built and remains invisible? We cannot currently tell the difference.

Restating the question
For us, Strategy A is not an option to be chosen. It is the present condition — already executed, and now approaching its ceiling.
The question is therefore not "A or B", but "what do we do with the time A has bought?"
05Strategy B is not a substitute for Strategy A — it is its precondition

Restate the absorptive-capacity ceiling from §3.3 in our own terms.

This framework measures capability on three levels (→ Part III, Ch. 13). The middle level — "Can evaluate", meaning a documented record of sending back, requesting changes to, or proposing alternatives to an external estimate or deliverable, with reasons — is the operational definition of absorptive capacity itself.

The most important consequence in this document
Strategy B is not an alternative to Strategy A. It is the precondition of Strategy A.
To keep buying from outside, someone inside must be able to judge whether what was bought is sound. Without that judgement, it is not procurement — it is delegation.
This is why "Can evaluate" sits at the centre of the framework: it is not an argument for insourcing, but the condition under which external dependence remains controllable.

The consequence has a practical corollary.

Secure the evaluation level and redundancy of execution can be bought externally. Placing people inside who can evaluate is cheaper — and more effective — than carrying a thick layer of in-house execution staff. This is examined against the alternatives in Part V, Ch. 31.

This document does not argue that external dependence should end. As §3.4 showed, that would mean giving up the ability to respond to discontinuous change. What it sets out is the condition under which external dependence stays controllable.
